> Adaptive learning adjusts the content, difficulty and order of a learning path to the learner's actual performance. Its cognitive foundations are among the best established in the learning sciences. But the effectiveness observed depends on the implementation, not the label. A system that genuinely measures acquisition and makes the learner do the work produces gains. A system that distributes content with an AI front end does not. Knowing how to tell the two apart avoids buying a promise in place of a mechanism.

### What is established

Three cognitive mechanisms underpin adaptive learning and are widely documented. Active retrieval: information is retained better when it is recalled from memory than when it is reread. Spaced repetition: reviewing information at increasing intervals consolidates it over the long term. The zone of proximal development: learning is more effective when the difficulty is slightly above the current level, neither too easy nor too hard.

These mechanisms are defined in detail in [What is adaptive learning and why it works](/referentiel/quest-ce-que-lapprentissage-adaptatif-et-pourquoi-ca-fonctionne). These principles predate AI. What AI brings is their implementation at scale. A human tutor can apply them for one learner. They cannot do so for hundreds at the same time. An adaptive system can, for each person, continuously.

### What the studies qualify

The documented learning gains vary widely between implementations. A system that merely dresses up content distribution with AI does not produce measurable gains. The quality of the underlying teaching content matters more than the sophistication on display. The effects are clearer for structured, assessable skills, and less well documented for behavioural and complex skills.

One point recurs in the literature: the sense of understanding that comes from reading an answer produced by an AI is misleading. Without the effort of retrieval, long-term retention remains low. An AI that makes the learner work improves results. An AI that works in the learner's place makes them worse.

### How to recognise a genuine adaptive system

Four questions help distinguish a mechanism from a façade. Is the personalisation recalculated at each step, or reduced to conditional paths defined in advance? Is the measurement of acquisition built into the learning path and spread over time, or limited to an end-of-module quiz? Does the system rely on a structured skills framework? Do the assessments measure the ability to use a skill, or only to recognise it?

A system that answers yes to all four acts on the content itself. A system that only adjusts the pace or the display is marketing.

### The conditions for success

Adaptive learning is not an end in itself; it is a function within a broader training system. Its effectiveness requires a solid skills framework, well-designed assessments that genuinely measure what they claim to measure, a learner who engages with the process rather than getting round it, and a context that allows time for training. The best algorithm running on a poor skills framework produces learning paths of little relevance.

### Frequently asked questions

**Is adaptive learning scientifically proven?**
Its cognitive mechanisms, active retrieval and spaced repetition, are among the best established. The evidence on specific implementations varies more from one system to another.

**How can you tell a genuine system from an AI façade?**
By checking that the personalisation is recalculated at each step, that acquisition is measured and spread over time, and that the system relies on a structured skills framework.

**Does adaptive learning work for all subjects?**
It is most effective for structured, assessable skills, and less suited to learning that requires a human judgement of quality, such as public speaking or leadership.

**Do you need to change your teaching approach to adopt it?**
Yes. Moving to adaptive learning requires rethinking assessments as learning mechanisms, not only as measurements.
