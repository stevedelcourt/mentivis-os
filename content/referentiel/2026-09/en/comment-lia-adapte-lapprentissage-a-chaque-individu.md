> AI adapts learning by adjusting, for each person, what is presented, in what order, at what level of difficulty and with what assessment. It relies on a diagnosis of the starting level, on continuous measurement of what has been learnt and on a skills framework that describes the target skills. What was once reserved for one-to-one tutoring becomes possible for hundreds of people at the same time. Adaptation is only valuable if it applies to content and difficulty, not only to pace or display.

### Starting from the actual level

Every adapted learning path begins with a diagnosis. Before offering any content, the system establishes what the person can already do, skill by skill, through questions and practical scenarios. This starting point avoids two costly mistakes: making the learner go back over what they already master, and confronting them with content too far from their level to be absorbed.

### Adjusting the difficulty

Learning is more effective when the difficulty sits slightly beyond the current level: enough to require effort, but not so much as to discourage. This is what the psychology of learning calls the zone of proximal development, described by Vygotsky. An adaptive system keeps the learner within this zone by raising the difficulty when answers are mastered, and by returning to prerequisites when a concept proves resistant.

### Choosing the order and the timing

Adaptation also applies to the sequence. A concept on which others depend is checked before moving on. Concepts already acquired come back at increasing intervals, following the principle of spaced repetition, which consolidates memorisation over time. The order of the learning path is therefore not fixed once and for all: it is recalculated as what has been learnt is confirmed or not.

### Making learners work rather than showing them

Assessments do not serve only to measure. Making yourself recall information from memory consolidates it more than rereading it: this is the active retrieval effect, documented notably by Roediger and Karpicke (2006). A system that adapts well asks questions, requires application, and has the learner explain. A system that merely supplies answers, however well phrased, gives an impression of understanding without producing lasting memorisation. The point is developed in [Learning with AI: what the research really says](/referentiel/apprendre-avec-lia-ce-que-les-etudes-disent-vraiment).

### Supporting without replacing

A conversational agent can answer the learner's questions as they arise, rephrase an explanation, or offer an example drawn from their job. Its role is to support the effort, not to make it in the learner's place. The conditions for this support are described in [How an AI agent supports a learner without replacing them](/referentiel/comment-un-agent-ia-accompagne-un-apprenant-sans-le-remplacer).

### What adaptation does not do

Adaptation does not make up for a vague skills framework or poorly designed assessments: it adjusts a learning path towards a target, and the target still has to be defined. It is more effective for structured, assessable skills than for those that require a human judgement of quality, such as public speaking or leadership. And it assumes a learner who engages with the effort rather than getting round it.

### Frequently asked questions

**What does AI actually adapt in a learning path?**
The content presented, the level of difficulty, the order of concepts, the timing of reviews and the form of assessments, based on each person's measured learning.

**Does the learner stay in control of their learning path?**
Yes. They can ask their questions, request explanations and see their progress skill by skill. Adaptation guides the learning path without confining it.

**Do you need a lot of data to adapt a learning path?**
Above all, you need a structured skills framework and reliable assessments. The initial diagnosis and the answers given throughout the learning path provide most of the information needed.

**Does adaptation work for all subjects?**
It is more effective for structured, assessable skills. For behavioural skills, it complements human assessment without replacing it.
