> A business case for an AI training system rests on four elements in a specific order: quantifying the real cost of the current problem (production, delivery, employee time, non-compliance), projecting the measurable benefits (reduced training time, fewer repeated courses, OPCO gains, lower production costs), estimating the real costs of deployment (not only the platform, but integration, configuration and maintenance), and identifying the risks. The strength of the business case depends less on the sophistication of the financial model than on the quality of the baseline data.

### Step 1: quantify the current problem

This is the most neglected and the most important step. A sound business case compares the cost of the AI system with the total cost of the problem it solves, not simply with existing training spend.

The production cost of current training: the cost of design, development and revision per hour of content. Industry figures range from 5,000 to 30,000 euros per hour of e-learning depending on the level of interactivity.

The cost of employee time spent in training: the number of training hours per employee per year, multiplied by the average fully loaded hourly cost, multiplied by the number of employees concerned.

The cost of repeated courses: what proportion of training is taken twice or more by the same employees because what they learned was not retained? An estimate of 15 to 25% is documented in studies on workplace training.

The cost of non-compliance: regulatory training that expires without renewal, OPCO applications rejected for lack of traceability.

### Step 2: project the measurable benefits

Reduced training time: apply 20% (a low, cautious threshold) to the cost of employee time. Fewer repeated courses: estimate a 50% reduction in the cost identified in step 1. OPCO funding gain: the value of the additional funding made possible by improved traceability. Lower production costs: a range of 30 to 60% is documented in published cases. The value of skills progression on identified operational indicators.

### Step 3: estimate the real costs

The cost of the platform over the planned period (at least 3 years). The cost of integration with existing tools: ask for this explicitly, with contractual commitments rather than indicative estimates. This cost is frequently underestimated during selection. The cost of configuring the skills framework and the learning paths. The cost of training the administrator, trainer and manager teams. The annual cost of maintenance and development.

### Step 4: identify the risks

Risk of insufficient adoption: if employees do not use the system, the benefits do not materialise. Data quality risk: inaccurate or incomplete data produce incorrect adaptations. Vendor lock-in risk: can the data be exported in a standard format? Risk of non-compliance with GDPR and the AI Act.

### Frequently asked questions

**Is an external consultancy needed to build this business case?**
Not necessarily. An internal business case on a limited scope, using the organisation's real data, is often more credible than an external projection based on generic benchmarks.

**How should the uncertainty in the projections be handled?**
By working with low and high ranges for each benefit line, and by identifying the most sensitive assumptions.

**Who should own this business case internally?**
Ideally the HR director and the CFO together, with sponsorship from a member of the executive committee.
