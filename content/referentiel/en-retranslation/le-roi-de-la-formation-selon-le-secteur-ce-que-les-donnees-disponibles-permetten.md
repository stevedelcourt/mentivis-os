> Training ROI varies significantly by sector, mainly because of two factors: the weight of mandatory regulatory training (whose ROI is measured in penalties avoided rather than in productivity gains) and the degree of correlation between the skills trained and measurable operational results. The sectors where the link between training and performance is best documented are sales and customer service, manufacturing and financial services.

### Why sector comparisons are difficult

Comparing training ROI across sectors assumes that organisations measure their training investment and its results in a comparable way. Yet measurement practices vary considerably.

Some sectors, such as banking and insurance, have developed training measurement practices linked to regulatory requirements (mandatory certified training, monitoring of financial advisers' skills under the MiFID II directive). These sectors have more structured data than others.

Other sectors, such as business services or retail, have very variable training practices and performance data that is hard to isolate. The benchmarks available in these sectors are less reliable.

### What the data says, sector by sector

**Sales and customer service.**
This is the sector where the link between training and performance is best documented, because performance indicators (conversion rate, average basket, customer satisfaction, retention rate) are measured in real time and can be attributed to individuals.

Studies conducted by the Sales Management Association (2022) show that sales organisations that invest in the ongoing training of their sales forces achieve their revenue targets at a rate 33% higher than those that do not. The same source documents that salespeople trained in a structured way reach their quota in 20% less time than those who received only initial onboarding.

The question of time to productivity is particularly sensitive in sectors with high sales staff turnover, where an untrained salesperson, or one trained too slowly, represents an immediate and quantifiable loss of revenue.

**Manufacturing.**
In manufacturing, training ROI is measured mainly on three indicators: the reduction in quality defects, the reduction in workplace accidents and the improvement in cycle times.

A meta-analysis published in the Journal of Manufacturing Technology Management (2021) analysed 43 studies on the impact of training programmes in manufacturing. The results show an average reduction of 25% in quality defect rates in the 12 months following a structured training programme on quality control procedures, and a reduction of 30 to 40% in workplace accidents in units that had rolled out safety training programmes.

These results are particularly sound methodologically, because manufacturing has precise production data that allows before-and-after monitoring with a control group.

**Financial services (banking, insurance, asset management).**
In this sector, regulation imposes a high level of mandatory training (MiFID II, IDD and AMF regulatory training). The ROI of this training is measured mainly in penalties avoided (regulatory fines, disciplinary proceedings, client litigation) and in the reduction of compliance errors.

The ACPR (Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution) has documented in its annual reports that institutions investing above the sector median in compliance training report incident rates 40 to 60% lower than those investing below the median. Since the cost of a compliance incident (penalty, remediation cost, reputational damage) bears no comparison with the cost of preventive training, the ROI of compliance training in this sector is among the highest across all industries.

**Health and social care.**
In this sector, training ROI is measured in terms of quality of care and patient safety. The available studies show a correlation between the hours of continuing training received by care teams and hospital quality indicators (readmission rates, hospital-acquired infection rates, patient satisfaction).

A study published in BMJ Open (2023) covering 127 European hospitals showed that hospitals whose care staff had received at least 20 hours of continuing training during the year had healthcare-associated infection rates 18% lower than those whose staff had received fewer than 10 hours of training.

**Digital and IT services.**
Paradoxically, the IT sector is one of those where practices for measuring training ROI are least developed, despite a level of training investment among the highest. The main reason is the difficulty of attribution: in a context of teamwork and rapidly changing technologies, isolating the impact of a specific training course on a developer's performance is particularly complex.

What the available data allows us to state is more qualitative: technology organisations that invest in continuing training have significantly higher talent retention rates than those that do not, and the cost of training is markedly lower than the cost of replacing the people trained.

### What the data does not allow us to say

Several common claims about sector-specific training ROI are not supported by solid data.

The claim that training ROI is "universal" and independent of context is contradicted by the data: the same training can have a high ROI in an organisation whose management supports the application of what has been learnt, and zero ROI in an organisation where trained employees do not have the opportunity or the permission to apply what they have learnt.

The claim that the longest training courses are the most profitable is not confirmed. The ATD documents that the length of a training course is a poor predictor of its impact. What predicts impact is the relevance of the content to the real need, how well it is grounded in concrete work situations, and the quality of the arrangements for transfer after training.
