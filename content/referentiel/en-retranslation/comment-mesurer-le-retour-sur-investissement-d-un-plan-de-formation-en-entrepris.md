> The return on investment of training is measured using three main approaches. The Kirkpatrick model assesses four levels: reaction, learning, behaviour and results. The Phillips ROI model adds a fifth level, financial ROI expressed as a percentage. The marginal contribution method isolates the impact of training on a specific performance indicator while controlling for other variables. None of these methods is universal: the choice depends on the type of training, the data available and the audience to be convinced.

### Why measuring training ROI remains difficult

Measuring the ROI of training is one of the most discussed and least resolved topics in human resources management. The difficulty is not methodological: the tools exist. It is practical.

The first cause of difficulty is attribution. When a salesperson's sales rise by 15% after negotiation training, how much of that increase is due to the training, and how much to market trends, a change in client portfolio, or accumulated experience? Isolating the effect of training requires a control group that most organisations have not set up.

The second cause is time lag. The effects of training often show up at 6, 12 or 18 months. Corporate budget cycles are not calibrated for this kind of deferred measurement.

The third cause is internal politics. Measuring ROI means admitting that a training course did not work if the indicators do not move. Few organisations have the maturity to draw that kind of conclusion publicly.

### The Kirkpatrick model

Developed in the 1950s by Donald Kirkpatrick, this model remains the most cited reference in the literature on training evaluation. It structures evaluation into four levels.

Level 1: Reaction. Did participants enjoy the training? Immediate satisfaction questionnaires, completed at the end of a session, measure this level. It is the easiest level to measure and the least predictive of actual effectiveness.

Level 2: Learning. Did participants acquire the targeted knowledge, skills or attitudes? This level is measured by knowledge tests before and after the training (the pre-test / post-test method).

Level 3: Behaviour. Do participants apply what they have learned in their work? This level is measured 30, 60 or 90 days after the training, through managerial observation or structured self-assessment.

Level 4: Results. Did the training produce the results expected for the organisation (productivity, quality, safety, customer satisfaction)? This level is the hardest to measure and the most significant.

### The Phillips ROI model

Jack Phillips added a fifth level to the Kirkpatrick model: converting results (level 4) into financial value, to produce an ROI expressed as a percentage. The formula is: ROI (%) = [(net benefits of the training / total costs of the training) x 100].

Net benefits are the difference between the value produced by the training (productivity gains, fewer errors, lower turnover) and its total cost. Total costs include not only tuition fees but also the salary cost of training hours, logistical costs and evaluation costs.

In practice, this model is powerful for convincing executive committees, but it requires quantified data on behaviours and results that most organisations do not collect systematically.

### The marginal contribution method

This more empirical approach consists of isolating a key performance indicator linked to the objective of the training (error rate, processing time, sales volume, customer satisfaction score) and measuring how it changes before and after the training for the trained group, compared with a reference group that has not been trained.

It is easier to implement than the Phillips model on limited scopes, and produces more convincing evidence for operational managers, because it relies on business indicators they already know.

### Which method to choose?

For short, high-volume training (awareness, regulatory compliance), Kirkpatrick levels 1 and 2 are enough. The aim is to prove that the content has been understood, not that behaviours have changed.

For training with high business stakes (sales training, management training, technical training), Kirkpatrick levels 3 and 4 are necessary, with a measurement delay of at least 90 days after the training.

For transformation projects (rolling out a new tool, reskilling a team), the marginal contribution method is the most relevant because it links training directly to an operational indicator that management already tracks.
