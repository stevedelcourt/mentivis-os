> Studies on the effectiveness of AI in learning show positive results under specific conditions and null or negative effects in other configurations. The decisive variable is not the technology but the learning design: an AI that forces the effort of retrieval improves results, while an AI that does the work in the learner's place makes them worse. This finding has been robust in the scientific literature since VanLehn's work (2011) on intelligent tutoring systems, confirmed by the meta-analyses of the Institute for Education Sciences.

### What the positive studies show

Well-designed intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) produce documented and significant learning gains. Kurt VanLehn's meta-analysis (2011), covering several dozen comparative studies, established that the best ITS produce learning gains comparable to those of an individual human tutor, around 0.76 standard deviations above standard group teaching. This is what researchers call Bloom's "two sigma problem": individualised teaching produces results far superior to mass teaching, and ITS come close to it.

These gains are observed consistently on structured, assessable skills: mathematics, physics, languages, programming, science. They are observed in populations with stable learning conditions, that is, learners who have the time, access to the technology and sufficient motivation to engage with the system.

### What the studies qualify

The effects vary widely between implementations. A system that merely delivers video content with an AI interface on the front does not produce measurable gains compared with video content without AI. The quality of the underlying learning content matters more than technological sophistication: poor content distributed well by AI is still poor content.

The available meta-analyses on MOOCs show completion rates of between 3% and 15% and learning effects that vary widely according to participants' level of active engagement. Platforms that build in active retrieval mechanisms (questions, exercises, regular assessments) achieve significantly better results than those that mainly offer passive video.

The effects on complex and behavioural skills, such as leadership, interpersonal communication, creativity and conflict management, are much less well documented.

### What the studies warn about

Using generative AI to produce answers without any retrieval effort on the learner's part degrades learning. This is the most important finding, and the one most ignored in commercial messaging.

The mechanism has been documented since the classic work on the "testing effect" or "retrieval practice effect": forcing yourself to recall information from memory produces far better long-term retention than rereading or passive listening. Reading an answer generated by an AI is a form of passive rereading: the information goes in easily, but retention at 30 days is very low.

The sense of understanding that comes from reading a well-phrased answer from a language model is misleading. Researchers call this phenomenon the "fluency illusion": because the answer is fluent and coherent, the learner feels they have understood, whereas superficial understanding without the effort of recall does not produce lasting retention.

### How to read these studies rigorously

Four criteria make it possible to assess the quality of a study on the effectiveness of AI in training.

Funding. A study commissioned by an AI vendor on its own product does not carry the same weight as an independent study.

The control group. A study that compares "before AI" and "after AI" in the same group without a control group cannot isolate the effect of AI. A randomised controlled trial is the reference standard.

The scope of the population. Results for primary school pupils in mathematics in the United States do not transfer automatically to senior managers in professional training in France.

The measurement period. Short-term effects and effects at six months can diverge significantly. Studies that measure only immediate effects systematically overestimate the effectiveness of the programmes.

### Frequently asked questions

**Is AI more effective than traditional training?**
It can be, for certain types of learning under certain conditions. The relevant question is not AI versus traditional, but which combination for which objective and with which learning design.

**Where can serious studies be found?**
In peer-reviewed education science journals (Journal of Educational Psychology, Learning and Instruction), OECD publications and the meta-analyses of the Institute for Education Sciences. Vendor white papers without peer review are marketing material, not scientific evidence.

**Are the gains maintained over time?**
When the active retrieval mechanism is present throughout the learning path, the gains are maintained at six months. When content is consumed passively, retention collapses within a few weeks, following the Ebbinghaus curve.
