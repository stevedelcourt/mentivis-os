> Adaptive learning is a teaching approach that adjusts the content, difficulty and progression of a learning path to the learner's observed performance and needs, in real time. It rests on three mechanisms drawn from cognitive science: active retrieval (we remember better what we recall than what we reread), spaced repetition (reviewing at increasing intervals consolidates long-term memory), and calibrating difficulty to the learner's actual level. AI makes it possible to apply these principles at scale, where they were previously limited to individual tutoring.

### The cognitive mechanisms it relies on

**Active retrieval.**
The testing effect, documented since the work of Roediger and Karpicke (2006) and confirmed by dozens of subsequent studies, establishes that we remember information better by recalling it from memory than by rereading it. The difference in long-term retention between the two methods is estimated at 50% in favour of active retrieval in the available meta-analyses.

Adaptive learning builds in frequent assessments that force this retrieval rather than leaving it optional. Assessments are not only measures of progress: they are learning mechanisms in their own right.

**Spaced repetition.**
The spacing effect, first described by Ebbinghaus in 1885 and extensively replicated since, establishes that reviewing information at increasing intervals consolidates it in long-term memory more effectively than concentrated revision. Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve shows that without review, 50% of information is forgotten within 24 hours and 80% within a week.

An adaptive system can automatically schedule the optimal moments for review for each learner on each skill. This is a task that cannot be done manually at scale.

**The zone of proximal development.**
Theorised by Vygotsky in the 1930s, the zone of proximal development describes the space between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with appropriate guidance. Learning is most effective when the difficulty is slightly above the current level. Content that is too easy produces no learning. Content that is too difficult produces frustration and drop-out. Continuous adaptation keeps the learner within this productive zone.

### Why it works at scale with AI

A human tutor can apply these principles to individuals. For cohorts of hundreds or thousands of learners, that is impossible. AI can do it. It assesses each learner at each step, schedules the optimal reviews, adjusts the difficulty of the next content according to results, and runs this cycle in real time for each person independently.

This is the democratisation of individualised tutoring that Bloom called the "two sigma problem": individualised teaching produces results two standard deviations above standard group teaching, and until now there was no way to deploy it at scale.

### The conditions for success

A sound skills framework. An adaptive system is only as precise as the framework it relies on.

Well-designed assessments, which genuinely measure what they are meant to measure. An assessment that tests recognition is different from one that tests production. Both have their place, but they do not measure the same thing.

A learner who engages with the process. An adaptive system can be circumvented if the learner answers at random to move ahead quickly.

### Frequently asked questions

**Is adaptive learning scientifically proven?**
The cognitive mechanisms it relies on (active retrieval, spaced repetition) are among the best established in the learning sciences. The evidence on specific implementations varies more from one system to another.

**Is it suitable for all types of content?**
It is particularly effective for structured, assessable skills. It is less suited to learning that requires a qualitative assessment of judgement or creativity.

**Does deploying it require a change in teaching approach?**
Yes. Moving to adaptive learning means rethinking assessments (designing them as learning mechanisms, not only as measures) and the skills framework.
