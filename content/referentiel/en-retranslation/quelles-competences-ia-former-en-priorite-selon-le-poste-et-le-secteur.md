> The AI skills to develop as a priority depend on the role, not the sector. Three levels structure a relevant AI training plan: basic AI literacy (understanding what a language model is, what it can and cannot do) for everyone; prompting skills and critical evaluation of AI outputs for regular users; and AI governance and deployment skills for decision-makers. The sector of activity shapes the concrete use cases, not the core skills.

### The initial mistake: AI training "for everyone"

The first generation of corporate AI training plans consisted of sending all employees to the same general session on "AI and ChatGPT". The result was consistently disappointing: too theoretical for operational staff, too superficial for technical profiles, and unconnected to the company's real use cases.

Effective AI training starts from real tasks, not from tools. The question is not "how do I use ChatGPT?" but "which tasks in my daily work can AI handle in full or in part, and with what precautions?"

### The priority matrix by profile

**Profile 1: all employees.**
Priority skills: understanding what a language model is and why it sometimes produces errors (hallucination), identifying tasks suited to AI in one's daily work, knowing which data must not be entered into an external LLM (customer data, personal data, confidential information), knowing how to check an AI output before using it.
Recommended format: a 2-hour awareness session, online or in person.

**Profile 2: regular users (marketing, communications, HR, administration, finance).**
Priority skills: writing effective prompts (basic prompt engineering), evaluating the quality and reliability of an AI output, advanced use of the tools built into business software (Copilot in Office, AI in CRMs, assistants in HR tools), knowledge of algorithmic bias and its impact on decisions.
Recommended format: 4 to 6 hours of training, differentiated by business area.

**Profile 3: technical and data teams.**
Priority skills: fine-tuning and adapting models, integrating LLM APIs into existing systems, evaluating and benchmarking models, managing the quality of training data, security and governance of AI pipelines.
Recommended format: a learning path of 20 to 40 hours, in several modules.

**Profile 4: decision-makers (HR directors, CEOs, executive committee, business line directors).**
Priority skills: understanding the real capabilities and limits of AI (without overselling or underestimating), the applicable regulatory framework (AI Act, GDPR, employment law), a method for identifying and prioritising use cases, AI governance (steering committee, usage policy, responsibilities).
Recommended format: two half-days, focused on the concrete decisions the organisation has to make.

### The sector dimension: what changes by industry

The sector of activity does not change the fundamental skills, but it determines the use cases in which the training must be anchored.

In the financial sector, the priority use cases are document analysis (reports, contracts, due diligence), anomaly detection and report generation. The specific risks to address in training are bias in scoring models and the traceability obligations for automated decisions.

In healthcare and social care, the use cases are summarising records, support for administrative writing and research assistance. The risks are critical: confidentiality of patient data, reliability of medical information generated by AI, and the responsibility of the healthcare professional.

In retail and distribution, the use cases are personalising offers, analysing customer data and generating product content. The risks concern the protection of customer data and the transparency of algorithmic recommendations.

In industry and B2B services, the use cases are technical writing (specifications, inspection reports), document management and support for technicians in the field. The risks concern the confidentiality of technical data and the reliability of the technical information generated.

### How to build the AI training plan from this matrix

Step 1: map the profiles present in the organisation and the number of people in each profile.
Step 2: identify the 3 to 5 most time-consuming tasks for each profile and assess which are candidates for AI.
Step 3: select the tools that match these tasks within the company's environment.
Step 4: build the learning paths by anchoring each module in a real use case, not in a tool.
Step 5: document the training delivered for AI Act compliance.
