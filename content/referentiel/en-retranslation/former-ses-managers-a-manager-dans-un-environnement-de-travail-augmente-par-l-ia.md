> The deployment of AI in organisations creates a blind spot in most training plans: employees are trained to use AI tools, but managers are not trained to manage teams that use AI. Yet AI fundamentally changes the nature of certain managerial tasks: assessing individual performance, allocating work, detecting errors, developing employees' skills. Managers who have not updated their frame of reference on these subjects make decisions on grounds that have become inaccurate.

### What AI changes in the manager's work

AI increases the productivity of some employees asymmetrically. Two employees at the same level of seniority and with identical job descriptions can have very different levels of productivity depending on how well they use AI tools. This asymmetry creates fairness and performance management challenges that managers have not yet learned to handle.

AI makes certain tasks almost invisible. When a task that used to take 3 hours is cut to 20 minutes thanks to AI, the time freed up is not automatically reallocated to higher-value tasks. Without a manager who asks how the freed-up time is used and who creates the conditions for that time to be reinvested productively, the potential productivity gain remains theoretical.

AI raises new questions of accountability. When an employee makes a decision based on a recommendation from an AI system, who is responsible for the decision? The employee, the manager, or the organisation that deployed the tool without training its teams to assess AI outputs critically? These questions are not settled by legislation in 2026, and managers need to take clear positions on the subject within their teams before an incident forces them to improvise.

AI raises questions of fairness in the allocation of work. If an employee has mastered AI and produces in 4 hours what their colleagues produce in a day, the manager has to decide: do I give them more work (and how do I recognise this productivity gap)? Do I train the other employees to close the gap? Do I reconfigure the roles?

### The specific managerial skills to develop

**Assessing the quality of AI outputs.** A manager who cannot recognise a hallucination in an AI-generated report, or who cannot assess the quality of a prompt used by their employee, is in fact delegating their capacity for oversight to the tool. Training managers to assess AI outputs critically is a prerequisite for any deployment of AI in a team.

**Redefining performance objectives in an augmented context.** When AI absorbs part of an employee's work, performance indicators have to change. What counts is no longer the volume produced, but the quality of the decisions made, the relevance of the prompts used, and the value of the work of supervising and enriching AI outputs. Managers need to learn to define and measure this new form of performance.

**Managing resistance and anxiety related to AI.** Not all employees experience the deployment of AI in the same way. Some embrace it as a productivity lever. Others experience it as a threat to their job or as a challenge to the value of their expertise. The manager is on the front line in supporting these reactions, and can only do so effectively if they themselves have a clear framework on the real stakes and impacts of AI on their team's roles.

**Maintaining the human connection in an increasingly automated environment.** Paradoxically, AI increases the importance of certain distinctly human managerial skills: the ability to build trust, to detect weak signals of demotivation or difficulty, to give meaning to organisational decisions. These skills are not new, but they become differentiating as routine tasks are automated.

### A training format suited to managers

Managers resist long training away from the work context more than other populations do. Three formats work better than others for this population.

Short sessions anchored in real situations (2 to 3 hours, based on cases experienced in the team). The manager brings a concrete situation they have faced, and the trainer uses it as material to draw out general principles that can be applied.

Peer co-development. Groups of 6 to 8 managers meet regularly to share their experiences of managing in an AI environment and to build answers together to the situations they encounter. This format draws on the collective intelligence of the group and produces answers grounded in the organisation's specific context.

Reverse mentoring. An employee who is proficient with AI tools supports their manager in getting to grips with the tools and in understanding their implications. This format has the advantage of creating a dialogue between generations and of recognising skills that are not always acknowledged in the traditional hierarchy.

### What does not work

AI management training that consists of explaining to managers how "AI will transform the world of work" without grounding it in their concrete situation has two negative effects. It generates anxiety without providing the tools to respond to it. And it is perceived as disconnected from operational reality, which discredits the HR director who rolls it out.

Managers need answers to very concrete questions: how do you assess the performance of an employee who uses AI intensively? How do you talk to an employee who refuses to use the AI tools deployed by the organisation? How do you set objectives that take account of AI-related productivity gains without penalising those who are least proficient with these tools?

Effective AI management training answers these questions with concrete frameworks for action, not with broad trends.
