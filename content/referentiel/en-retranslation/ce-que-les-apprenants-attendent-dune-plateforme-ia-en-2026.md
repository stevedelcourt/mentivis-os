> In 2026, learners arrive at training with expectations shaped by their everyday use of consumer AI assistants. A Microsoft France and YouGov study from January 2026 indicates that more than 7 in 10 managers and professionals use an AI tool in their personal or working lives. These users expect from their training platforms what they find in those tools: an immediate answer, content calibrated to their actual level, and visible progress. Conventional e-learning platforms do not meet these expectations, and this translates directly into dropout rates.

### How learners' frame of reference has changed

The spread of consumer AI assistants since 2023 has changed learners' frame of reference with regard to digital learning tools. Someone used to obtaining a personalised, immediate answer on any subject perceives differently a platform that imposes a linear learning path identical for everyone, with 45-minute video modules and a multiple-choice quiz at the end of each module.

This shift changes the threshold of tolerance for friction and the subjective definition of "what is useful". A training course that would have seemed reasonable in 2020 can feel outdated to the same learner in 2026, simply because their frame of reference has moved on.

### What learners want

An immediate answer to their questions, without waiting for the next webinar or the trainer's next message. Waiting 48 hours for an answer to a comprehension question interrupts the momentum of learning, and this frequently results in dropout.

A learning path that takes account of what they already know. Repeating fundamentals they have already mastered is the main cause of frustration identified in surveys on dropout from online training.

Content perceived as relevant to their actual situation. Genericness is the second most frequently cited reason for dropping out. A module with no connection to the employee's specific context is perceived as standard content, not as training.

Visible progress, skill by skill. Abstract completion percentages do not produce engagement. Progress defined in terms of skills acquired is perceived as real value.

### What learners do not want

Long video modules with no interactivity. An adult's tolerance for passive video in a work setting is estimated at between 6 and 12 minutes.

Identical multiple-choice quizzes for everyone at the same moment. Standardised assessment shows the learner that the system is not trying to find out their level.

Complex interfaces. Every additional step is a potential point of friction.

Monitoring that feels like surveillance rather than support.

### What this means for organisations

Platforms whose experience resembles the digital tools learners use in their personal lives show higher measured engagement rates. Friction in use is as important a cause of disengagement as the quality of the content.

### What this does not mean

That learners' expectations define what they should learn. A highly engaging platform delivering the wrong content produces learners who are satisfied but not very competent.

### Frequently asked questions

**Do expectations vary between generations?**
Yes as regards interfaces, less so on substance. All learners, whatever their generation, respond better to content they perceive as relevant and to a pace suited to their actual level.

**Should learners be consulted before choosing a platform?**
Yes. A demonstration to a panel of 5 to 10 representative learners reveals usability obstacles that decision-makers do not see on their own.

**How can engagement be measured without confusing it with acquisition?**
By strictly separating indicators of logins and completion (engagement) from indicators of measured acquisition (progress on the target skills).
