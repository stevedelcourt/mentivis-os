> Some training is required by law or by sector-specific regulations, regardless of the employer's wishes. Failing to provide it exposes the company to criminal penalties, administrative closure or civil liability in the event of an accident. The most heavily regulated sectors are construction and public works, road transport, healthcare, the chemical and food industries, and private security.

### What the law says

Article L. 6321-2 of the Code du travail requires employers to provide the training their employees need to adapt to their jobs. It distinguishes training that is a condition for carrying out an activity or function (mandatory training in the strict sense) from training that relates to keeping employees employable.

Mandatory regulatory training is training without which the employee is not entitled to carry out their activity, or which exposes the company to a criminal offence if absent. It must take place during working time and be paid as such. Its cost is borne entirely by the employer and cannot be charged to the employee's CPF account.

### Construction and public works: the most demanding training requirements

The construction and public works sector accounts for the largest number of regulatory training requirements.

Electrical authorisation (standard NF C 18-510) is mandatory for any employee required to carry out work on or near electrical installations. It is issued by the employer after training and assessment, and must be renewed periodically.

CACES training (Certificat d'aptitude à la conduite en sécurité, a certificate of fitness for safe operation) is required to operate site machinery (cranes, forklift trucks, aerial work platforms). The CACES is issued for 5 or 10 years depending on the category.

SST training (Sauveteur Secouriste du Travail, workplace first aid) is mandatory in workshops where hazardous work is carried out, and strongly recommended throughout the sector. It must be refreshed every 24 months.

Asbestos training is mandatory for any operator, supervisor or contracting authority working on materials likely to contain asbestos. The level required varies according to the type of work.

### Road transport: FIMO and FCO obligations

In road haulage and passenger transport, the Formation Initiale Minimale Obligatoire (FIMO, mandatory minimum initial training) is required for every professional driver. It lasts 280 hours for new drivers.

The Formation Continue Obligatoire (FCO, mandatory continuing training) is the renewal of this qualification, every 5 years, with 35 hours of training. A driver without an up-to-date FCO is legally unfit to work.

### Healthcare: obligations linked to risks and procedures

In the health and social care sector, mandatory training requirements are numerous and regularly updated.

Emergency procedures training is mandatory for care assistants and nurses. Training in the prevention and control of healthcare-associated infections (PRÉVIAS) is required by hospital regulations. Training in good care practice and the prevention of abuse is required in establishments that accommodate vulnerable people.

### Chemical and food industries

Workers exposed to hazardous chemical agents (ACD) must have received training in chemical risk prevention, in accordance with Articles R. 4412-38 et seq. of the Code du travail. The same obligation applies to carcinogenic, mutagenic and reprotoxic agents (CMR).

In the food industry, HACCP training (hazard analysis and critical control points) has been mandatory for anyone responsible for placing foodstuffs on the market since the law of 27 July 2010.

### Private security

The private security sector requires a professional licence issued by the CNAPS (Conseil national des activités privées de sécurité, the national council for private security activities), which depends on passing initial training and regularly updating one's knowledge.

### What the employer risks in the event of a breach

Failure to comply with mandatory regulatory training exposes the employer to several types of sanction. Under criminal law, the absence of training can constitute inexcusable fault in the event of a workplace accident, giving rise to an increase in the pension paid to the victim and to a recourse action by the CPAM against the employer. Administratively, certain breaches can lead to the closure of the establishment or the withdrawal of an operating licence. Under civil law, the employer can be held directly liable by the injured employee or by third parties.
