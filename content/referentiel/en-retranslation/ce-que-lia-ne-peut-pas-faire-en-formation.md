> Current AI systems cannot assess skills that require sound human judgement, handle complex emotional situations, guarantee the reliability of what they generate without expert validation, or make up for failing organisational conditions. These limits are not temporary flaws awaiting correction: they are structural. Ignoring them leads to deployments that produce disillusionment and remediation costs.

### Why identifying the limits is a condition of success

Enthusiasm for AI in training produces two opposite kinds of error. Rejection without assessment: "the human relationship is irreplaceable, AI cannot train people." Over-expectation without discernment: "AI will revolutionise everything, trainers will disappear."

Both positions overlook the same thing: AI performs well on certain types of task and poorly on others, in a predictable and documented way. Identifying precisely what it can and cannot do makes it possible to use it where it delivers real value, and to keep human mechanisms where they are irreplaceable.

### What AI cannot do

**Assess skills that require sound human judgement.**
Public speaking, managerial stance, the ability to handle conflict, creativity in the moment, active listening in an advisory relationship: these skills are assessed through human observation and cannot be reduced to indicators that can be automated. An AI system can assess whether a learner has retained the stages of a commercial negotiation. It cannot assess whether that learner builds the relationship of trust needed for a negotiation to succeed.

This limit has a concrete implication: training programmes on behavioural and interpersonal skills require human assessment mechanisms to be kept in place, whether observation by a trainer, role plays with human feedback, or peer assessment.

**Replace human support in complex learning.**
An expert trainer brings contextual judgement about the learner's specific situation, an ability to detect what the learner does not put into words, and a relationship that motivates persistence in the effort. A line manager who gives feedback in real working situations produces learning that AI cannot reproduce.

**Guarantee the quality of what it generates without human validation.**
Language models produce content that is fluent, apparently coherent, and factually incorrect with a far from negligible frequency. AI-generated content on a regulatory, technical or medical subject must be reviewed and validated by an expert. Generation is not verification.

**Solve an organisational problem on its own.**
If employees do not have time to train, if management does not value skills development, if learners have no opportunity to apply what they have learned: the best AI system produces no result. AI optimises the training programme; it does not create the organisational conditions in which training can have an effect.

### What this means for deployment

Successful deployments deliberately combine two types of mechanism: AI mechanisms for what they do well (personalisation, generation, measurement, continuous availability, traceability), and human mechanisms for what AI cannot do (assessment of behavioural skills, support in complex learning, validation of content on sensitive subjects, the organisational conditions for transfer).

The temptation to remove human mechanisms in order to cut costs is the main cause of failure in AI deployments in training.

### Frequently asked questions

**Will AI replace trainers?**
No, for trainers who work on complex, situational and behavioural learning. Partly, for the production and delivery of standardised content on structured skills.

**Can we do without subject matter experts to validate AI-generated content?**
No, for technical, regulatory or sensitive fields. Expert validation remains necessary before any deployment, and periodic review is required.

**Does AI work better for certain types of training?**
Yes. It is more effective on structured skills that can be assessed: procedural knowledge, regulations, languages, mathematics, use of tools.
