> The skills development plan (PDC) is the document through which the employer organises training for its employees. All companies are required to ensure that their employees adapt to their jobs and to maintain their ability to hold employment. This obligation applies regardless of company size. The absence of a formal PDC is not penalised directly, but it exposes the employer to risks in the event of an inspection or an employment tribunal dispute.

### What is the skills development plan?

The skills development plan is the direct successor to the training plan, which it replaced following the 2018 reform of vocational training (the law of 5 September 2018 on the freedom to choose one's professional future). It brings together all the training actions the employer decides to implement for its employees, whether these actions are mandatory or at the employer's initiative.

Two categories of action coexist in the PDC.

Mandatory training actions are those that are a condition for carrying out an activity or function, pursuant to an international convention or to statutory and regulatory provisions. They take place during working time and are paid as such. The employer bears their full cost.

Non-mandatory training actions are those the employer chooses to fund to develop its teams' skills, without being required to by any text. They may take place outside working time, subject to conditions and with the employee's agreement.

### Who is concerned?

All companies, regardless of size or sector. The obligation of adaptation and of maintaining employability is set out in Article L. 6321-1 of the Code du travail. It applies to the employer from the first employee.

In practice, companies with fewer than 50 employees are not required to consult their social and economic committee (CSE) on the PDC, unlike companies with 50 or more employees, which must submit the PDC to the CSE for consultation every year.

### What are the risks without a PDC or without training?

The absence of a formal plan is not a criminal offence in itself. However, the employer is exposed to three distinct types of risk.

Employment tribunal risk is the most common. A dismissed employee can invoke the employer's failure to meet its obligation of adaptation if they received no training during their contract. The courts have regularly awarded damages on this basis, particularly where the employee shows that their dismissal is linked to skills obsolescence that the employer did not address.

Risk during an OPCO review is secondary but real. When a request for training funding is made, the OPCO may examine whether the request is consistent with the company's training policy. A complete lack of structure can weaken funding applications.

The risk linked to the professional development review is the most quantified. An employee who has not received at least one non-mandatory training action over six years and has not had a professional development review every two years is entitled to a corrective top-up of their personal training account (CPF) of 3,000 euros, payable by the employer. This point is covered in detail in the article on the professional development review.

### How to structure an effective PDC

An operational PDC has five components.

An inventory of roles and required skills. This means identifying, role by role, the skills needed to perform the function today and over the next 12 to 24 months. It is the starting diagnosis, without which the plan is blind.

Identification of gaps. Once the required skills are listed, the gap between the employee's current level and the expected level makes it possible to prioritise training actions.

Selection of training actions. Each action chosen must be linked to a specific objective and to an identified role. A training catalogue with no grounding in a skills framework produces training plans that are costly and have no measurable impact.

The schedule and the provisional budget. The PDC is a management document, not a wish list. Without a schedule and an associated budget, it remains a formality.

The evaluation process. Each training action must be followed by an assessment of learning, formal or informal, making it possible to measure whether the skills gap has actually been reduced.

### The most common mistakes

Confusing the PDC with a training catalogue. A catalogue is a list of available offerings. A PDC is an action plan grounded in the company's real needs. This confusion is the leading cause of training plans with no measurable return on investment.

Ignoring mandatory regulatory training. Some training is required by sector-specific texts (electrical authorisations, safety training, training on plant protection products, chemical risk training) and its absence exposes the employer to criminal penalties independent of the PDC.

Not involving managers. A PDC built solely by HR without input from the front line is invariably disconnected from real needs. Operational managers are the first to see the skills gaps in their teams.

Forgetting to document. In a dispute, what counts is proof that the training took place, not the intention. Every training action must be recorded: invitation, programme, attendance sheets, evaluation.
