> Article 4 of the European regulation on artificial intelligence (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, known as the AI Act) became applicable on 2 February 2025. It requires providers and deployers of AI systems to take the measures necessary to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff. The obligation applies regardless of size: an SME with five employees using ChatGPT for work is covered in the same way as a large industrial group. National authorities, including the CNIL in France, have been able to carry out inspections and impose sanctions since 2 August 2026.

### Who is covered?

The regulation distinguishes between providers (those who develop AI systems or place them on the market) and deployers (those who use AI systems in a professional context). Article 4 applies to both categories.

The notion of deployer is deliberately broad. It covers any professional use of an AI system, including the use of consumer tools such as Copilot, Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini at work. A communications agency that generates visuals with an AI tool, an accountancy firm that uses a writing assistant, or an HR department that uses an AI-powered CV screening tool all fall within its scope.

There is no size threshold. The European Commission explicitly confirmed this point in its official FAQ on AI literacy, published in 2025.

### What does the AI literacy obligation involve in practice?

The text of Article 4 refers to "appropriate measures taking into account the context of deployment". It does not prescribe a specific training format or a minimum number of hours. What is expected is an approach that is proportionate to the role and the risk.

In September 2025 the European Commission published guidance that sets out the expected level according to three profiles.

The occasional user (an email writing assistant, occasional use of a chatbot): an awareness session of 1 to 2 hours covering how language models work, the notion of hallucination, the data that must not be entered into a public LLM, and the basic rules of use.

The regular user (content production, task automation, data analysis using AI): training of 4 to 6 hours, differentiated by business area, also covering algorithmic bias, risks relating to personal data and human oversight of AI outputs.

The power user or decision-maker (deploying an AI system, overseeing an AI project, making decisions based on AI outputs): a certified learning path, with specific modules on AI governance, the applicable regulation and legal responsibilities.

### The AI Act timetable and the situation in September 2026

The regulation follows a phased timetable.

1 August 2024: official entry into force of the regulation.

2 February 2025: Article 4 (AI literacy) becomes applicable, together with the ban on unacceptable AI practices (Article 5).

2 August 2025: Member States were required to notify the Commission of the national penalties they had set for breaches.

2 February 2026: obligations for general-purpose AI models (GPAI).

2 August 2026: national authorities obtain their full powers of inspection and sanction. In France, the government tabled an amendment in February 2026 designating the CNIL as the national supervisory authority for the AI Act. The CNIL therefore now has considerably extended powers of inspection and sanction.

2 August 2027: full obligations for all AI systems, including high-risk systems.

### What penalties?

Article 4 itself does not set a penalty of its own. However, failure to comply with it is an aggravating factor in any inspection relating to an AI incident. The general penalty regime set out in Article 99 of the regulation provides for administrative fines of up to 15 million euros or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

These ceilings are adjusted downwards for SMEs. Fines are not automatic and take into account the nature, gravity and duration of the breach, whether it was intentional or negligent, and the measures taken to mitigate the harm.

An important point noted by several lawyers: a GDPR inspection by the CNIL can now be widened to include a check on AI Act compliance. Evidence of AI training may be requested in the same way as evidence of GDPR compliance.

### What compliance requires in practice

Four elements make up the minimum expected during an inspection.

An inventory of AI use within the organisation: who uses what, for which tasks, on which data. This document is also used for classification by risk level.

An internal policy on the use of AI tools: authorised tools, prohibited data (sensitive personal data, client confidential information, health data), a requirement for human review before publication or decision, and a procedure for reporting an incident.

Training activities differentiated by profile, documented and traceable: invitations, programmes, attendance sheets or certificates of completion.

A person designated to monitor AI Act compliance, even in small organisations.

### The role of the CSE

For companies with at least 50 employees, the introduction of an AI system with a significant impact on working conditions or on the management of jobs must be the subject of a consultation with the social and economic committee (CSE). This consultation is separate from the training obligation but complements it: an AI deployment project that is documented and submitted to the CSE demonstrates serious governance in the event of an inspection.
