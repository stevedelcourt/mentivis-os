> Learning science is unambiguous on this point: learning the same amount of content in several short sessions spaced out over time produces significantly better long-term retention than equivalent intensive training. The spacing effect, documented since Ebbinghaus's work in the nineteenth century and confirmed by hundreds of studies since, is one of the most robust findings of cognitive psychology applied to learning.

### The spacing effect: what the research says

The spacing effect was first described by Hermann Ebbinghaus in his work on memory, published in 1885. It has been confirmed and refined by several hundred studies since.

The simplest way to put it: if you have to learn X in 6 hours, a single 6-hour session will produce significantly lower retention at 30 days than three 2-hour sessions spread over 3 weeks, with identical content.

Robert Bjork, a cognitive psychology researcher at UCLA, has documented what he calls "desirable difficulties": learning conditions that seem harder in the short term (shorter sessions, spacing, recall tests) in fact produce more lasting retention in the long term, even though learners perceive them as less effective during the training itself.

### Why do organisations prefer the intensive format?

Intensive training is preferred for logistical reasons, not educational ones. It is easier to schedule (employees' absences are concentrated in one period), it is simpler to contract with a provider (a two-day session is easier to invoice than a programme of 10 sessions of 45 minutes), and it gives the impression of a visible investment that is limited in time.

These advantages are real, but they concern the organisation of training, not its educational effectiveness.

### Microlearning: the right answer to the right question

Microlearning, which consists of delivering training in very short modules (3 to 10 minutes), takes advantage of the spacing effect and fits around employees' availability. It is effective for factual knowledge, procedures and compliance reminders.

It is less effective for complex skills that require reasoning, deliberate practice and feedback in context. Training people in sales negotiation through 5-minute modules is insufficient; keeping the steps of a sales process fresh in memory with 2-minute reminders after initial training is appropriate.
