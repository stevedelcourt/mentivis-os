> A training policy aligned with business strategy starts from two simple questions: what skills must the organisation have in 18 to 36 months to execute its strategy? And what skills does it have today? The gap between these two answers defines the training plan. Most training policies fail because they start from the available catalogue rather than from real needs.

### Why most training plans do not serve the strategy

In most organisations there is a structural disconnect between strategic planning processes and training planning processes. Strategy is set at executive committee level, generally over 3 to 5 years. The training plan is built by the HR department or the training department, generally over 12 months, from managers' feedback and a catalogue of providers.

These two processes rarely meet. The result is a training plan that reflects the individual preferences of managers and the offers available on the market, rather than the organisation's transformation priorities.

### Step 1: start from the strategy to identify the skills required

Each strategic priority of the organisation implies specific skills. A strategy of digitalising processes implies skills in change management, in the use of digital tools and in data analysis. An internationalisation strategy implies language, intercultural and regulatory skills. A product innovation strategy implies skills in design thinking, agility and managing uncertainty.

The first step is to read the organisation's strategic plan and extract from it the 5 to 10 skills on which its execution will depend.

### Step 2: assess the skills already present

Once the required skills have been identified, the organisation's current level in each must be assessed. This assessment can take several forms: interviews with managers, structured self-assessment by employees, knowledge or skills tests, or analysis of HRIS data (mobility, promotions, appraisal review results).

The aim is not precision but the identification of risk areas. Which strategic skills are missing or insufficient? How many employees need them?

### Step 3: build the plan from the gaps

The gap between required skills and present skills automatically generates the priorities of the training plan. Every training action must be justifiable by its link to an identified gap.

This logic profoundly changes the way training budgets are defended. Rather than justifying a budget against the previous year or against a sector benchmark, the HR director can justify every euro of training by its link to an identified strategic risk.

### Step 4: measure and adjust

A training policy aligned with the strategy must be evaluated on the same indicators as the strategy itself. If the strategic objective is to increase international revenue by 30% in three years, the training indicators must include the number of employees trained in languages and international sales practices, and the change in performance of the teams concerned.
