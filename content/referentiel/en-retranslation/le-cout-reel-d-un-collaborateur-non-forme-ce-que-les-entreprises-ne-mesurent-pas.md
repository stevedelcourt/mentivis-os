> The cost of an untrained employee is not limited to visible errors. It includes chronic underproductivity (estimated at 10 to 20% of working time according to studies on engagement), the cost of errors and rework, the increased likelihood of voluntary departure (employees who receive no training are between 30 and 60% more likely to leave the company), and the cost of replacement (estimated at between 50% and 200% of annual salary depending on the role).

### Why this cost remains invisible

Company accounts easily record the costs of training: tuition fees, trainers' costs, the salary cost of hours not worked. These costs are visible, budgeted and often challenged when budgets are tight.

The costs of not training, on the other hand, have no line in the accounts. They are hidden in other categories: quality costs (rework, scrap, customer complaints), HR costs (recruitment, onboarding), operational costs (longer lead times, dependence on internal experts).

This accounting asymmetry creates a systematic bias in budget decisions: the cost of training is certain and immediate, while the cost of not training is diffuse and deferred. Decision-makers rationally choose to cut the visible cost.

### The five components of the cost of not training

**Chronic underproductivity.** An employee who lacks the right skills for their role works more slowly, produces lower-quality outputs, and takes up more of their colleagues' and manager's attention. Studies on workplace engagement (Gallup, 2024) estimate that employees who feel insufficiently equipped for their tasks have a level of engagement between 20 and 40% lower than those who feel competent.

**The cost of errors and rework.** In sectors with strict quality constraints (industry, healthcare, finance, legal), the cost of an error caused by a lack of training can far exceed the cost of the training that would have prevented it. Fixing a software bug costs on average 10 times more if it is detected in production rather than in development. The same principle applies to most professional errors.

**Accelerated turnover.** Several meta-analyses have established a negative correlation between investment in training and the rate of voluntary departure. Gallup has documented that employees who have had development opportunities in the last 12 months are between 30% and 60% less likely to be actively looking for another job. Conversely, the lack of development prospects is regularly cited among the top three reasons for leaving in exit surveys.

**The cost of replacement.** Replacing an employee costs between 50% and 200% of their annual salary, depending on the level of qualification and the scarcity of the profile. This cost includes recruitment fees (around 15 to 25% of annual salary if recruitment goes through an agency), the cost of onboarding (the new hire's salary during the period in which they are not yet productive), and the loss of knowledge caused by the departure.

**The cost of dependence on internal experts.** In organisations where training is not structured, critical skills are often concentrated in a small number of employees. This concentration creates an operational dependence: if these employees leave or are absent, the organisation is stuck. The cost of this vulnerability is difficult to quantify in advance, but obvious afterwards.

### What the research says

The meta-analysis by Tharenou et al. (2007), covering 67 empirical studies, established that investment in training is positively correlated with individual performance in 58% of the studies analysed, and with organisational performance in 44% of the studies. These figures are cautious: the authors stress that correlation does not prove causation and that the quality of training is decisive.

A study by ATD (Association for Talent Development, 2023) showed that organisations that invest heavily in training have 218% higher revenue per employee than those that invest little, and 24% higher profit per employee. These figures should be interpreted with caution (the organisations that invest in training are also those that have the means to do so), but they reflect a robust trend.
