> The forgetting curve established by Hermann Ebbinghaus at the end of the nineteenth century remains valid: without review or application, 50% of what has been learnt in training is forgotten within 24 hours, and 80% within a week. The main cause of failure in workplace training is not the quality of the trainer but the absence of any arrangement for consolidation and application after the session. Learning design is the discipline that designs training arrangements which take these constraints into account.

### The problem with full-day training

The dominant format of professional training in France is still the full day in a classroom, or its e-learning equivalent: a long module, followed by a test, followed by forgetting. This format is designed for logistical convenience, not for learning.

Over the past 30 years, learning science (cognitive science, educational psychology) has produced a body of convergent findings on what supports retention and the transfer of skills. Most workplace training practices ignore these findings.

### What the research says about learning

**The spacing effect.** Learning the same thing in several sessions spaced out over time produces significantly better retention than one long session with equivalent content. Six hours of training spread over 3 sessions of 2 hours across 3 weeks is more effective than a continuous 6-hour day.

**The testing effect (retrieval practice).** Forcing yourself to recall information from memory (by answering questions, by solving exercises) is more effective for retaining it than rereading it or listening to it again. Consolidation quizzes are not gimmicks: they are the most effective means of embedding what has been learnt.

**Learning in context.** Knowledge learnt in an abstract context is difficult to transfer to a concrete one. Training grounded in the participant's real work situations is consistently more effective than generic training.

**Cognitive overload.** The human brain has a limited processing capacity. A module that presents too much new information in too little time produces cognitive overload that prevents retention. The densest training courses are often the least effective.

### The five most frequent causes of failure

No prior diagnosis. Training without having identified precisely what the participant already knows and what they do not yet know leads to repeating material already mastered and missing the real gaps.

No link with real work. Training whose content cannot be applied immediately in the participant's daily work is forgotten within a few days, whatever the quality of the trainer.

No follow-up after training. The training session is the beginning of a learning process, not its end. Without consolidation exercises, without opportunities for guided application and without feedback, the content fades quickly.

Training "too late". Training delivered too long before the need becomes active (training on a tool 6 months before it is rolled out) or too long after (training on a process 12 months after it was introduced) has little effect.

Confusing attendance with learning. Attending a training session does not guarantee learning. An employee who has "done" their day of training and signed the attendance sheet has not necessarily acquired the target skills. Measuring attendance instead of measuring what has been learnt is a design error.
