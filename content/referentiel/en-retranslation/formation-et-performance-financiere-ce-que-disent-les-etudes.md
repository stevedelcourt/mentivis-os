> Studies on the link between training and financial performance show a positive correlation, but one that is difficult to isolate causally. The most robust converging data come from ATD, McKinsey and the World Economic Forum: companies that invest systematically in skills development outperform their sectors on indicators of productivity, retention and capacity to innovate. The causal link remains debated, but the absence of training is documented as a factor of underperformance in organisations undergoing transformation.

### What McKinsey says

In 2021 the McKinsey Global Institute published a report on the future of work after the pandemic, which estimates that companies investing in training and internal redeployment of their workforce recoup their investment in under two years, through productivity gains and lower recruitment costs.

A complementary McKinsey study (2023) on "learning organizations" identifies five characteristics shared by the best-performing organisations in their sector: a rate of investment in training above the median, training programmes anchored in strategic priorities, a systematic culture of feedback, regular measurement of skills, and visible leadership commitment to development issues.

### What Deloitte says

Deloitte's Human Capital Trends measures HR practices in several thousand organisations worldwide every year. The 2022 to 2025 editions converge on one point: organisations that invest in a "skills-based approach" (managing by skills rather than by jobs) report higher levels of performance than those that manage HR through the traditional organisation chart.

According to the 2024 edition, "skills-based" organisations are 63% more likely to achieve their performance objectives, and 57% more likely to adapt quickly to market changes.

### What ATD says

The Association for Talent Development publishes its "State of the Industry" report every year, benchmarking training practices in several hundred American and international organisations. The 2023 data show that organisations with high training performance invest on average 1,252 dollars per employee per year in training, compared with 805 dollars for the average. They also show a voluntary turnover rate 25 to 30% lower than the average for their sector.

### The methodological limits to be aware of

These data must be interpreted with caution, for three reasons.

Selection bias is significant. The organisations that invest heavily in training are also, in general, those that have the financial means to do so and an advanced management culture. It is difficult to distinguish the effect of training from the effect of corporate culture.

Correlation is not causation. No study proves that training causes performance. The studies show that the two frequently coexist, which is consistent with the causal hypothesis but does not demonstrate it.

The quality of training is decisive. Investing in training is not enough. Training that is poorly designed, disconnected from business issues or not followed by an opportunity to apply it has little or no effect on performance. Studies that measure the ROI of "training" in general mix investments of very variable quality.
