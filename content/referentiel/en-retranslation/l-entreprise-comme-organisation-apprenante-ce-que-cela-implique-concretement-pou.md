> A learning organisation is one that has developed the ability to learn collectively from its experience, adapt its practices and generate new knowledge continuously. It is not a state reached once and for all, but an organisational capability that is built and maintained. In practice, it requires four kinds of infrastructure: processes for sharing experience, safe spaces for questioning, real-time skills management, and leadership that models learning rather than delegating it.

### Peter Senge and the fifth discipline

Peter Senge introduced the concept of the learning organisation in his book "The Fifth Discipline" (1990). In it he describes five disciplines that characterise organisations able to learn: personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning, and systems thinking (the "fifth discipline", which integrates the other four).

In organisational practice, the concept has remained largely theoretical. HR directors who refer to it often do so to describe a general cultural ambition, not a concrete programme of action.

### What it means in practice

**Processes for sharing experience.** A learning organisation has formal mechanisms for capturing and disseminating what its members have learned from their experience, including from their failures. Structured lessons-learned reviews (REX) after projects, communities of practice and cross-team sharing sessions are examples of these mechanisms.

**Safe spaces for questioning.** An organisation can only learn if its members are able to voice doubts, identify dysfunctions and suggest improvements without fear of negative consequences. Psychological safety (a term introduced by Amy Edmondson in her work on high-performing teams) is a prerequisite for organisational learning.

**Real-time skills management.** A learning organisation knows at any moment which skills it has, which are changing, and which it lacks. This real-time visibility requires skills mapping tools, connected to HR processes and updated regularly.

**Leadership that models learning.** The behaviour of leaders sends the most powerful signals about an organisation's culture. A leader who speaks publicly about their own learning and mistakes, takes part in training, and values questioning over certainty creates the conditions for a learning organisation. A leader who delegates training entirely to HR sends the opposite signal.
